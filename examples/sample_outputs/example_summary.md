# The Future of AI: World Models, Open Research, and the Path Beyond LLMs

## Metadata

- Processed: 2025

## Overview

In this wide-ranging conversation at Davos, AI pioneer Yann LeCun challenges prevailing assumptions about artificial intelligence, arguing that current LLM-based approaches are fundamentally limited and that achieving human-level intelligence requires a paradigm shift toward "world models" that understand physical reality. LeCun advocates forcefully for open-source AI development, warns against the concentration of AI power, and presents his vision for "objective-driven AI" systems that can plan, reason, and predict consequences—capabilities he argues are impossible with language models alone. He also dismisses apocalyptic AI narratives as distractions from real near-term risks like centralized control of AI systems.

## Main Themes

### Theme 1: The Fundamental Limitations of LLMs and the Need for World Models

[00:00:04 - 00:04:51]

LeCun presents a provocative critique of the current AI paradigm, arguing that large language models represent a dead-end for achieving true intelligence. His central thesis is that LLMs cannot support genuinely intelligent behavior because they lack the ability to predict consequences of actions—a capability he considers essential for planning and reasoning.

He illustrates this limitation with a compelling analogy: "How can a system possibly plan a sequence of actions if it can't predict the consequences of its actions?" [00:01:36]. This inability to model cause and effect means that "basing agentic systems on LLMs is a recipe for disaster" [00:01:30]. LeCun argues that we're already seeing the limits of the LLM paradigm, despite widespread industry enthusiasm for agentic AI systems.

The solution, according to LeCun, lies in "world models"—systems that can build predictive models of how their environment will evolve and understand their effect on that environment. He emphasizes that real intelligence comes from understanding the physical world, not language: "The real world is way more complicated than the world of language" [00:03:19]. Paradoxically, while humans consider language the pinnacle of intelligence, "predicting the next word in the text is not that complicated" [00:03:31].

LeCun provides concrete evidence of this gap through examples of human learning efficiency versus AI: a 17-year-old can learn to drive a car within 10 hours, while autonomous vehicles require "millions of hours of training data" and still haven't achieved level five autonomous driving [00:02:27]. Similarly, a 10-year-old can solve simple tasks on the first try without training—what he calls "zero shot task solving"—something current AI systems cannot do [00:02:15].

Key insights:
- LLMs fundamentally cannot plan because they cannot predict action consequences
- Intelligence requires understanding the physical world, which is far more complex than language
- Current AI architectures are orders of magnitude less sample-efficient than human learning
- "World models" that predict environmental changes are essential for genuine intelligence

Notable quote: "You're not going to get intelligent behavior without that. You're not going to get efficient learning without that. You're not going to get zero shot task solving." [00:02:07] - LeCun on the necessity of world models for achieving real AI capabilities.

### Theme 2: Open Source AI as Essential Infrastructure and Democratic Imperative

[00:04:51 - 00:07:19, 00:16:06 - 00:17:59]

LeCun makes an impassioned case that openness in AI research has been the single most important driver of progress and that the recent trend toward closed, proprietary systems threatens both innovation and democracy. He identifies open research culture—not any particular technical breakthrough—as "the biggest factor in progress" [00:04:26].

Drawing on historical parallels, LeCun notes that "historically platforms have always become open source" [00:13:22], citing how the entire internet infrastructure runs on Linux and open-source software despite early proprietary alternatives. He predicts AI will follow the same trajectory, becoming fundamental infrastructure that cannot be controlled by a handful of companies.

The political and cultural stakes are even higher than the technical ones. LeCun warns that "the biggest risk of AI" is not machines taking over the world but rather that "in the near future where our entire digital diet will be mediated by AI systems if those AI systems come from a handful of proprietary companies on the west coast of the US or China, we're in big trouble for the health of democracy, cultural diversity, linguistic diversity, value systems" [00:15:36]. He advocates for "a highly diverse population of AI assistance for the same reason we need diversity in the press" [00:16:00].

To his dismay, major AI labs are increasingly closing up: "OpenAI, Anthropic was never open in fact very closed, Google became slightly open and now more closed" [00:06:19]. Meanwhile, "the best open source models at the moment come from China" [00:06:51], creating a competitive disadvantage for the West. LeCun proposes a consortium where "various regions in the world will contribute to training a global open-source LLM that could constitute the repository of all human knowledge" [00:15:08].

Key insights:
- Open research culture, not specific inventions, drove AI's rapid progress
- AI is becoming platform infrastructure, which historically always becomes open source
- Centralized control of AI systems poses the greatest near-term risk to democracy
- No single company can create systems representing all human knowledge and cultural diversity
- China's open-source AI models are gaining adoption while Western labs close

Notable quote: "We need a highly diverse population of AI assistance for the same reason we need diversity in the press and that can only happen with open source." [00:16:00] - LeCun on why open AI is essential for democratic society.

### Theme 3: The JEPA Architecture and Physical AI Revolution

[00:07:19 - 00:11:51]

LeCun provides detailed insight into his new venture, Advanced Machine Intelligence (AMI, pronounced "ami" meaning "friends" in French), which is developing a fundamentally different AI architecture called JEPA (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture). This represents his vision for what he calls "the physical AI revolution" [00:04:49].

JEPA differs fundamentally from generative AI models. Instead of generating outputs in pixel or word space, it "makes predictions in a representation space" [00:10:19]. The system learns by training on unlabeled video data to predict missing parts and develop an understanding of physical reality. LeCun explains: "We have systems now that we can train completely self-supervised on unlabeled videos and those systems understand video represent it really well can predict missing parts in a video and they also have acquired a certain sense of common sense" [00:09:31].

The proof of concept is striking: "If you show them a video where something impossible happens they tell you this is impossible" [00:09:50]. For example, if you throw a ball in the air and it stops or disappears, "prediction error goes to the roof because the system says like no this is completely incompatible with what I've observed during my training" [00:10:01].

This non-generative approach is crucial because "generative architectures do not work with this kind of data" when dealing with "high dimensional continuous noisy" sensory information like video [00:03:51]. LeCun envisions these systems building "phenomenological models of complex systems"—digital twins of industrial processes, chemical plants, jet engines, or even living cells [00:11:06]. The key insight is that accurate prediction requires abstraction: "If you simulate a system too accurately you can't predict anything" [00:11:53]. He uses the example that while you could theoretically explain everything in a room using quantum field theory, "that would be completely impractical" [00:12:08]—psychology and social science provide better predictive models at the appropriate level of abstraction.

Key insights:
- JEPA makes predictions in representation space rather than generating pixels or words
- Systems trained on video develop common sense about physical impossibilities
- Non-generative architectures are necessary for handling continuous, high-dimensional sensory data
- Effective prediction requires finding the right level of abstraction, not maximum detail
- Applications include digital twins of complex industrial and biological systems

Notable quote: "The next revolutionary AI which is coming fast is going to be AI systems that understand the real world. Systems that understand high continuous noisy data like video, like sensor data." [00:04:06] - LeCun on the coming shift from language-based to physically-grounded AI.

### Theme 4: Rethinking AI Risks and Rejecting Apocalyptic Narratives

[00:16:06 - 00:17:59, 00:18:41 - 00:20:35]

LeCun forcefully dismisses existential AI risk narratives as "BS if you pardon my French" [00:17:27], arguing they distract from genuine near-term concerns. When asked about risks like AI taking over and "killing us all," he responds: "That's BS... we had a debate on this two years ago" [00:17:24].

Instead, he identifies concentration of power as the primary risk: "The most important risk of AI is that in the near future where our entire digital diet will be mediated by AI systems if those AI systems come from a handful of proprietary companies... we're in big trouble" [00:15:36]. This isn't a hypothetical future threat but an immediate concern about who controls the information infrastructure of society.

On economic displacement, LeCun cites prominent economists including Nobel Prize winner Daron Acemoglu and Erik Brynjolfsson, who predict AI will "improve productivity by something like 6% per year" [00:18:04]—significant but not catastrophic. Importantly, "it's not going to create major unemployment like mass unemployment" [00:18:25] because "what limits the speed at which the technology disseminates in the economy is how fast people can learn to use it. So it's sort of a built-in regulatory mechanism" [00:18:31].

LeCun also reframes the alignment problem, arguing that current approaches focused on making LLMs behave properly are misguided because "we can never be sure that an LLM would behave properly because the data that we train it on is a very small subset of all the prompts that people can feed it" [00:20:03]. His alternative vision involves "objective-driven AI"—systems given specific objectives subject to "guard rails which have to be satisfied at inference time" [00:19:50], providing structural rather than probabilistic safety.

He dismisses the notion of sudden AI takeover: "A lot of people have in their mind that there's gonna be one secret to AGI whatever they call it and the next day computers are going to take over the world. This is ridiculous. It never happens this way" [00:25:32]. Instead, progress comes through "a bunch of conceptual breakthroughs which are going to be in obscure research papers that nobody is going to pay attention to until five years later" [00:25:46].

Key insights:
- Existential AI risk narratives are unfounded distractions from real problems
- Concentration of control over AI systems poses the greatest immediate risk
- Economic displacement will be gradual (6% productivity growth annually), not catastrophic
- Human learning speed acts as a natural regulator of AI adoption
- Current LLM alignment approaches cannot guarantee safety
- Objective-driven architectures with structural guardrails offer better safety
- AI progress is gradual and incremental, not a sudden "event"

Notable quote: "There's going to be a bunch of conceptual breakthroughs which are going to be in obscure research papers that nobody is going to pay attention to until five years later when someone demonstrates how powerful they are." [00:25:46] - LeCun on how AI progress actually happens.

### Theme 5: Education, Fundamentals, and Preparing for an AI Future

[00:21:01 - 00:22:34]

LeCun offers concrete advice for students and workers navigating rapid technological change, emphasizing the critical importance of learning fundamentals over trendy skills. His core message is that "technology progress is accelerating and what that means is that everyone who is studying right now is going to have to change job because technology evolves so quickly" [00:21:16].

The solution is to focus on "things that have a long shelf life will not be out of fashion in five years or 10 years. Very fundamental thing" [00:21:35]. He provides a specific example: "If you have the choice between taking a course in I don't know mobile app programming or quantum mechanics take quantum mechanics even if you're a computer scientist" [00:21:49]. The reasoning is that "the methods that you will learn doing this will allow you to learn to learn and also you'll have basic techniques you can reuse in all kinds of different contexts" [00:22:02].

LeCun illustrates this with an unexpected connection: "How would you know in advance that all the underlying mathematics of machine learning basically comes from statistical physics right which is why there are so many physicists who do AI these days" [00:22:11]. This demonstrates how fundamental knowledge in seemingly unrelated fields provides the conceptual tools for emerging technologies.

His three-part framework for students is: "Learn fundamentals, learn to learn and then be ready to change expertise to change job" [00:22:25]. This reflects a shift from career stability to continuous adaptation, where the ability to acquire new expertise becomes more valuable than any particular skill set.

Key insights:
- Career-long learning and job changes are now inevitable due to accelerating technology
- Fundamental knowledge has longer shelf life than specific technical skills
- Deep study of fundamentals teaches "learning to learn"
- Seemingly unrelated fields (physics, mathematics) provide crucial conceptual tools
- Adaptability and continuous re-skilling are essential career strategies

Notable quote: "If you have the choice between taking a course in I don't know mobile app programming or quantum mechanics take quantum mechanics even if you're a computer scientist because the methods that you will learn doing this will allow you to learn to learn." [00:21:49] - LeCun's advice to students on prioritizing fundamentals.

## Key Takeaways

1. **LLMs are fundamentally limited**: Current language model architectures cannot achieve human-level intelligence because they cannot predict action consequences, plan effectively, or learn efficiently from limited data. A paradigm shift to world models is necessary.

2. **World models are the next frontier**: AI systems must learn to understand and predict the physical world through video and sensory data, not just language. JEPA (Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture) represents this new approach.

3. **Open source is essential for democracy**: The greatest near-term AI risk is not machines taking over but centralized control of AI systems by a few companies, which threatens cultural diversity, democratic values, and information freedom.

4. **Open research drives progress**: The single most important factor in AI's rapid advancement has been open research culture—sharing papers, code, and models—not any particular technical breakthrough.

5. **China leads in open-source AI**: While Western labs increasingly close their research, Chinese organizations are producing the best open-source models, creating competitive and geopolitical implications.

6. **Economic impact will be gradual**: AI will likely improve productivity by ~6% annually—significant but not catastrophic. Human learning speed naturally regulates adoption, preventing mass unemployment.

7. **Apocalyptic AI narratives are distractions**: Fears of AI extinction events are unfounded and distract from real concerns about power concentration, misuse, and equitable access.

8. **Intelligence requires physical grounding**: Real intelligence comes from understanding the messy, high-dimensional physical world, not the relatively simple domain of language prediction.

9. **Learn fundamentals, not trends**: Students should prioritize deep foundational knowledge (mathematics, physics, core principles) over trendy technical skills, as they'll need to change careers multiple times.

10. **AI progress is incremental, not sudden**: There will be no single "AGI moment." Progress comes through obscure research breakthroughs that take years to demonstrate their full impact, as happened with deep learning and transformers.
